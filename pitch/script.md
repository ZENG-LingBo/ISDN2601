# Speaking script: “We should hang out.”

ISDN 1001 · Individual project · Problem-statement pitch
Live, 26 Nov 2026, 9:00 a.m. · **Time limit: 2:00. Aim for 1:50.**

The script is about 240 words when the numbers are read out in full. At a calm 135 words a minute, with the short pauses marked below, that's about 1:50. The same words are in each slide's speaker notes. Time yourself: if a run goes over 1:52, use the cuts at the end.

---

## Slide 1: The group chat (0:00–0:13)

*(Show the first slide. Take one breath and look at the room before you start.)*

> “We should hang out soon!” Four thumbs up. Everyone agrees. Then… nothing.
> Three months later, it’s still the last message in the chat.

*Read the chat message as if you sent it. Slow down on “Then… nothing.”*

## Slide 2: Target user (0:13–0:27)

> Meet Mandy, a Year 2 student at HKUST. In Year 1 she made four close friends. Now they have
> different timetables. She misses them, but no one ever picks a date.

## Slide 3: Background (0:27–0:45)

> Mandy is not alone. This year, 83.7% of young people in Hong Kong reported moderate to high
> loneliness. And 68% of UK adults aged 18 to 34 admit they cancel plans at the last minute.

*Say “eighty-three point seven percent” slowly. The 2021 figure and the US number are on the slide, so you don't need to say them.*

## Slide 4: Say yes, then regret it (0:45–0:57)

> Here’s the strange part. We say yes because saying no feels rude. When a friend cancels, one in
> three of us feels relieved… and then guilty.

*Smile a little on “relieved”. Most people in the room know this feeling.*

## Slide 5: Why plans fall through (0:57–1:12)

> Why do plans fall through? No one is in charge. The plan has no set time or place, just “soon”.
> And we’re afraid of looking too eager, but friends want to hear from us more than we think.

*Count the three reasons on your fingers.*

## Slide 6: Empathy map (1:12–1:24)

> So Mandy says “Next week for sure!”, likes the message, and waits. She thinks, “If I keep
> asking, I’ll seem desperate.” She feels lonely, then relieved, then guilty.

## Slide 7: Problem statement and How Might We (1:24–1:52)

> My problem statement: university friend groups want to spend their free time together, but
> their plans fall through in the group chat.
>
> **How might we turn “we should hang out” into real meetups for university friend groups,
> in order to keep their friendships close?**
>
> *(Pause.)* Everyone wants to meet. No one is in charge of the plan.

*Slow down for the question. Stop after the last line and wait. Don't add “that's it” or “any
questions?” The last line is your ending.*

## Likely questions, with short answers

**“Isn't most of your cancelling data from the UK and US?”**
Yes. The loneliness numbers are from Hong Kong: the HKFYG survey of 600 young people in 2026, and the Hong Kong Family Wellbeing Index 2026. The numbers on cancelled plans are from UK and US polls, because I couldn't find a Hong Kong survey on that. That gap is why my next step is to interview HKUST students.

**“Why not just use a WhatsApp poll?”**
This pitch is only about the problem. A poll could help people pick a time, but it doesn't fix the other two reasons: no one is in charge of the plan, and people are afraid of looking too eager. I want to keep the question open to many ideas.

**“Isn't this just laziness?”**
The research says no. People underestimate how much their friends like them (Boothby et al., 2018) and how much friends want to hear from them (Liu et al., 2023). People do care. They worry about how they'll look, and no one takes charge.

**“Why university students, and why Year 2?”**
Year 1 has ready-made chances to meet, like orientation camp, halls and shared classes. By Year 2, everyone has a different timetable, but the friendships are still new. Close friendships need about 200 hours together (Hall, 2019), so this is when missed plans hurt the most.

**“Is Mandy real? Did you talk to users?”**
Mandy is a persona built from the research, and the slide says so. My next step is interviews with HKUST students. *(If you did the friend check tonight, add your real result here.)*

**“Darley and Latané studied emergencies, not group chats.”**
Yes. Their study shows diffusion of responsibility: the more people who could act, the less each one feels it's their job. I'm applying that idea to group chats.

**“What's your solution?”**
Today is only about the problem. I kept the question open on purpose, so the answer could be a space, an event or a group habit, not only an app. *(Don't show or mention any prototype.)*

**“Maybe people just don't want to meet?”**
More people are disappointed than pleased when plans are cancelled: 36% against 17% (YouGov, 2023). People want to meet, but the plan falls through.

**“How was the 83.7% measured?”**
It's from an online survey of 600 people aged 12 to 24 by the Hong Kong Federation of Youth Groups, using the UCLA loneliness scale. It isn't a random sample.

**If someone asks about the 86% guilt figure:** it's about people who cancel, not people who feel relieved when a friend cancels. “Relieved, then guilty” describes the persona's feelings; if pressed, say that.

**“How does this fit ‘Leisure in 2030’?”**
The brief asks how free time can move from killing time to meaningful downtime. Right now, students often spend that time alone on their phones. This project is about helping friends spend that time together.
