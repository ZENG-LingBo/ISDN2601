# Speaking script: "We should play sometime."

ISDN 1001 · Individual project · Problem-statement pitch
**Time limit: 2:00. Aim for about 1:50, and finish by 1:52.**

The script is 240 words, or about 245 when the numbers are read out in full. At a calm 135 to 140 words a minute, with the short pauses marked below, that's about 1:48 to 1:52. The same words are in each slide's speaker notes. Time yourself: if a run goes over 1:52, use the cuts at the end.

The research behind every number is in [`research/sport-background.md`](../research/sport-background.md).

## Slide 1: Mandy at 15 and at 20 (0:00–0:16)

*(Show the first slide. Take one breath and look at the room before you start.)*

> At 15, Mandy played basketball three times a week. School gave her the time, the team, the court and the coach.
> At 20, she hasn't played in a year, but she still says, "We should play sometime."

*Slow down on the four things school gave her. They come back on slide 4.*

## Slide 2: Target user (0:16–0:19)

> Mandy is my target user.

*Point to the persona and pause for a moment. The slide shows the rest. Don't read the pain points aloud.*

## Slide 3: Activity drops when school ends (0:19–0:39)

> Even at school, 81% of students aged 11 to 17 don't get enough activity. Leaving high school is linked to about 7 fewer active minutes a day.
> And in Hong Kong, 53.8% of adults miss the WHO target.

*Say "eighty-one percent" and "fifty-three point eight percent" slowly.*

## Slide 4: Three things a game needs (0:39–0:58)

> On her own, a game needs three things: a group that's free at the same time, a court that bots haven't booked, and the nerve to be the weakest player.
> People picked last at school say they're less active as adults.

*Count the three things on your fingers.*

## Slide 5: The phone is always there (0:58–1:18)

> Meanwhile, about 80% of Hong Kong secondary students spend 2 hours or more on screens for fun on a school day.
> A phone is fun in seconds, and no one sees you lose.
> *(Pause.)* It isn't laziness. Starting a game is harder than staying on your phone.

## Slide 6: Empathy map (1:18–1:24)

> So Mandy says, "We should play sometime," and opens her phone.

## Slide 7: Problem statement and How Might We (1:24–1:52)

> Problem statement: university students want to play together, but every game needs a group that's free, a booked court and the nerve to be the weakest player. So they stay on their phones.
>
> **How might we make starting a game together as easy as opening a phone, for university friend groups, in order to help them move and laugh together more often?**

*Slow down for the question. Stop after it and wait. Don't add "that's it" or "any questions?"*

## Likely questions, with short answers

**"Isn't this just laziness?"**
The research says the main reason people give is lack of time, not lack of interest (LCSD, 2023; Silva et al., 2022). My point is that starting a game takes a lot of planning, while a phone takes none. When one choice is that much easier, people take it, even if they'd enjoy the other one more.

**"Why university students? The 81% number is about school students."**
That number shows that even with school's help, most teenagers don't move enough. The drop comes when school ends: about 7 fewer active minutes a day after high school (Winpenny et al., 2020). In Hong Kong, people aged 15 to 24 had the biggest fall in daily activity from 2015 to 2022 (Population Health Survey, via HKBU). University is the first time students have to set everything up themselves.

**"Is Mandy real? Did you talk to users?"**
Mandy is a persona built from the research, and the slide says so. My next step is to interview HKUST students. I've written the questions already. *(If you talked to friends, add your real result here.)*

**"Isn't the 80% screen number about secondary students, not university students?"**
Yes. It's the closest Hong Kong number I found. It shows the habit students bring with them to university. I haven't found a Hong Kong figure for university students yet.

**"Is court booking really a problem?"**
The LCSD found people using bots to book courts on SmartPLAY, and now bans those accounts for 360 days. In 2024, three suspected court scalpers were arrested at the Victoria Park tennis courts. I haven't found numbers on how many bookings fail, so that's one of my interview questions.

**"What about cost or injury?"**
I looked at both. Public courts in Hong Kong are cheap, and cost isn't one of the top reasons in the LCSD survey. Only about 1 in 10 students give fear of injury as a reason. So I focused on time, place and confidence, which are the reasons people give most.

**"The picked-last study is old and from the US."**
Yes, and it's a link, not proof of a cause. People remember being picked last and report being less active later. Two reviews of young people dropping out of sport point the same way: personal and social reasons matter more than practical ones (Crane and Temple, 2015; Back et al., 2022).

**"What's your solution?"**
Today is only about the problem. I kept the question open on purpose, so the answer could be a new game, a place, a club habit or an app.

**"Why 'as easy as opening a phone'? That's very hard."**
It's meant to be. The phone is what students choose instead of playing, so that's the bar any idea has to meet.

**"How does this fit 'Leisure in 2030'?"**
The brief asks how free time can move from killing time to meaningful time, with more live, in-person experiences. Right now, students who used to play together spend that time alone on screens. This project is about getting them playing together again.
