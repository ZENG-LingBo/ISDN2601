# Speaking script: "We should play sometime."

ISDN 1001 · Individual project · Problem-statement pitch
**Time limit: 2:00. Aim for about 1:50, and finish by 1:52.**

The script is 229 words, or about 233 when the numbers are read out in full. At a calm 135 words a minute, that's about 1:44 of speech. The other 6 to 8 seconds are for the pauses marked below and for changing slides. The same words are in each slide's speaker notes. Time yourself: if a run goes over 1:52, use the cuts at the end.

The research behind every number is in [`research/sport-background.md`](../research/sport-background.md).

## Slide 1: Mandy at 15 and at 20 (0:00–0:15)

*(Show the first slide. Take one breath and look at the room before you start.)*

> At 15, Mandy played basketball three times a week. School gave her the time, the team, the court and the coach.
> At 20, she hasn't played in a year.

*Slow down on the four things school gave her. Three of them come back on slide 4.*

## Slide 2: Target user and empathy map (0:15–0:30)

> Mandy is my target user. She misses the team, but she worries she'll play badly in front of friends.
> So she says, "We should play sometime," and opens her phone.

*Point to "Says" when you quote her.*

## Slide 3: Activity drops when school ends (0:30–0:50)

> She's not alone. When school ends, activity drops: about 7 fewer active minutes a day after high school.
> In Hong Kong, 53.8% of adults miss the WHO target, and lack of time is the top reason.

*Say "fifty-three point eight percent" slowly.*

## Slide 4: Pain points (0:50–1:03)

> Without school, a game needs three things: a time when everyone's free, a court she can actually book, and the nerve to be the weakest player.

*Count the three things on your fingers. The slide shows the evidence for each one, so you don't need to read it.*

## Slide 5: The phone is always there (1:03–1:25)

> Meanwhile, the phone is always there. 4 in 5 Hong Kong secondary students spend 2 hours or more on screens for fun on a school day.
> *(Pause.)* Laziness is only part of it. Starting a game is harder than staying on your phone.

## Slide 6: Problem statement and How Might We (1:25–1:52)

> Problem statement: university students want to play together, but a game needs a time when everyone's free, a court and the nerve to be the weakest player. So they spend their free time on their phones.
>
> **How might we make starting an in-person game as easy as reaching for a phone, for university friend groups, in order to help them move and laugh together more often?**

*Slow down for the question. Stop after it and wait. Don't add "that's it" or "any questions?"*

## Likely questions, with short answers

**"Isn't this just laziness?"**
Laziness is one reason people give, along with time and tiredness (LCSD, 2023). My point is that a lot of what looks like laziness is effort. A game takes days to set up, and a phone takes seconds. When one choice is that much easier, people take it.

**"Why university students?"**
Leaving high school is when activity drops: about 7 fewer active minutes a day (Winpenny et al., 2020). In Hong Kong, people aged 15 to 24 had the biggest fall in daily activity from 2015 to 2022 (Population Health Survey, via HKBU). University is the first time students have to set everything up themselves.

**"Weren't students already inactive at school?"**
Many were. Worldwide, 81% of school students aged 11 to 17 don't get enough activity (Guthold et al., 2020). But school still gave them the time, the team and the court. After school, even that is gone, and activity drops further.

**"Is Mandy real? Did you talk to users?"**
Mandy is a persona built from the research, and the slide says so. My next step is to interview HKUST students. I've written the questions already. *(If you talked to friends, add your real result here.)*

**"Isn't the screen number about secondary students, not university students?"**
Yes, and I say so. It's the closest Hong Kong number I found. It shows the habit students bring with them to university. I haven't found a Hong Kong figure for university students yet.

**"Is court booking really a problem?"**
The LCSD found people using bots to book courts on SmartPLAY, and now bans those accounts for 360 days. In 2024, three people suspected of reselling court bookings were arrested at the Victoria Park tennis courts. I haven't found numbers on how many bookings fail, so that's one of my interview questions.

**"What about cost or injury?"**
I looked at both. Public courts in Hong Kong are cheap, and cost isn't one of the top reasons in the LCSD survey. Injury isn't a top reason in the surveys I found either. So I focused on time, place and confidence, which are the reasons people give most.

**"The picked-last study is old and from the US."**
Yes, and it shows a link, not proof of a cause. People who remember being picked last report being less active later. A review of young people dropping out of sport points the same way: personal and social reasons come up more often than practical ones (Crane and Temple, 2015).

**"What's your solution?"**
Today is only about the problem. I kept the question open on purpose, so the answer could be a new game, a place, a club habit or an app.

**"Why 'as easy as reaching for a phone'? That's very hard."**
It's meant to be. The phone is what students choose instead of playing, so that's the bar any idea has to meet.

**"How does this fit 'Leisure in 2030'?"**
The brief asks how free time can move from killing time to meaningful time, with more live, in-person experiences. Right now, students who used to play together spend that time alone on screens. This project is about getting them playing together again.
