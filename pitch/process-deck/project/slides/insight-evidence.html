New insight: the research behind it

The Abilene paradox

A group can agree to a plan most of its members don’t want, because each person thinks the others want it, and nobody says otherwise (Harvey, 1974).

People with food limits feel lonelier

Partly because they worry what others will think of them, and that they’ll hold up the group’s choice (Woolley et al., 2020).

Private facts don’t come up

Groups talk more about what everyone already knows than about what only one person knows (Stasser & Titus, 1985).

44%

of people with a food allergy who answered a survey wouldn’t always tell staff in a restaurant or café. Teenagers worried most about making a fuss (Food Standards Scotland, 2020).

70%+

of 445 young people in Hong Kong said dining out together was what they most wanted to do after COVID-19 rules ended (HKBU, 2020).

Limits: the 44% is from Scotland and is about restaurant staff, not friends. The HKBU survey was during the pandemic. Nothing I found measures this in Hong Kong friend groups, so it’s an interview question.

The Abilene paradox, from Jerry Harvey in 1974, describes a family that drives to Abilene even though none of them wants to go, because each one thinks the others do. That's the dinner nobody really wanted. Woolley and colleagues found that people with food limits feel lonelier, partly because they worry about how others see them. Stasser and Titus showed that what only one person knows rarely comes up in a group. A private food limit is that kind of fact. Two numbers support it, but both have limits, so this is a question for the interviews.
