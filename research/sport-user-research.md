# User research plan: why friends stop playing together

The pitch's problem statement and Mandy's persona come from published research. This plan checks them with real HKUST students, builds the personas from real people, and then tests Freeze with real groups. It follows the same steps as the Minibus study, in more detail.

## What to find out

1. Do students play sport or active games with friends less than they did at school? Which reasons matter most: time, a place, enough people, skill, cost or getting hurt?
2. What happens between someone saying "we should play sometime" and nobody playing?
3. How do students feel about playing with people who are better than them?
4. What do they do instead, and how do they feel about it?
5. Later, with Freeze: does a group start playing within a few minutes, and does everyone join in?

## Four studies, in this order

| Study | Who | Time | What it gives you |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Survey in the group chat | 20 to 40 students | 3 minutes each | Numbers: how often people play now, compared with at school, and the biggest reasons they don't |
| 2. One-to-one interviews | 5 to 8 students | 20 to 30 minutes each | Stories and quotes for the personas and empathy maps |
| 3. Friend-group session | 1 or 2 groups of 3 to 5 friends | 30 minutes | You watch a group try to plan a game, and see where it gets stuck |
| 4. Freeze playtest | 2 or 3 groups of 3 to 6 friends | 20 minutes each | Whether the game works, whether it's fun, and whether anyone sits out |

Do the survey first, because it's quick and its answers tell you what to ask about in the interviews. Do the playtest last. Don't show Freeze to anyone before their interview or group session, so they talk about their own life rather than your idea.

## Before you start

- **Consent.** Tell people what the study is for, that taking part is optional, and that you'll quote them without their name. Ask before you record anything, and delete recordings once you've typed your notes. Ask before taking photos or videos during the playtest.
- **Ask about real events.** "Tell me about the last time…" gives true answers. "Would you…?" gives polite guesses.
- **Don't lead.** Ask "What happened next?" rather than "Was it hard to find a court?". Let silences run, and people will often add the most useful part.
- **Friends are kind.** Say something like "The most useful thing you can tell me is what doesn't work." Then believe them when they say it.
- **Friends aren't everyone.** Ask each friend to forward the survey to one person outside your group. When you report the results, say they come from a small group of friends.

## 1. Survey

Make it in Microsoft Forms with your HKUST account, set it to anonymous, and post the link in the group chat. Keep the questions in this order: the questions that hint at a laptop game come last, so they don't change the earlier answers.

**Message for the group chat:**

> Hi all, I'm doing user research for my ISDN 1001 project, about how students spend their free time with friends. It's 14 quick questions, about 3 minutes, and anonymous. It would really help if you could also send it to one friend outside this chat. Thank you! [link]

**Questions:**

1. What year are you in? (Year 1 / Year 2 / Year 3 / Year 4 or above / Postgraduate / Other)
2. Where do you live during term? (Hall on campus / Flat off campus / Family home / Other)
3. In your last year of secondary school, how often did you play a sport or active game with friends, outside PE lessons and team training? (Never / Less than once a month / 1 to 3 times a month / Once a week / More than once a week)
4. In the last month, how often did you play a sport or active game with friends, outside PE lessons and team training? (Same choices as question 3)
5. When did you last play a sport or active game with friends, outside PE lessons and team training? (In the last 7 days / 1 to 4 weeks ago / 1 to 6 months ago / 7 to 12 months ago / Over a year ago / Never)
6. What stops you from playing with friends more often? Choose up to 3.
   - Finding a time when everyone's free
   - Finding or booking a place
   - Not enough people
   - Nobody organises it
   - I'm not good at it
   - I don't want to look bad in front of others
   - It costs money, or I'd need to buy things
   - I might get hurt
   - I'm too tired
   - I'd rather do something else
   - Nothing stops me
   - Other
7. In the last month, how many times did someone in a group chat suggest playing a sport or game, and it didn't happen? (0 / 1 or 2 / 3 to 5 / More than 5)
8. What did you mostly do last Saturday evening? (short answer)
9. How much do you agree: "I'm happy with how I spend my free time now." (1 Strongly disagree / 2 Disagree / 3 Neither / 4 Agree / 5 Strongly agree)
10. What was your average daily screen time on your phone last week? It's in your phone's settings. (Under 2 hours / 2 to 4 hours / 4 to 6 hours / Over 6 hours / I don't know)
11. Which is closer to you? (I'd like to play with friends more / I play about as much as I want / I'd like to play less)
12. How much do you agree: "I worry about being the worst player in the group." (1 Strongly disagree to 5 Strongly agree, as in question 9)
13. How often do you have your laptop with you on campus? (Almost always / Often / Sometimes / Rarely or never)
14. Where could you and 3 or more friends play something for 15 minutes without booking? Choose all that apply. (Hall common room / A bedroom / A living room / An empty classroom / Somewhere outdoors / Nowhere I can think of / Other)

At the end, add a line: "Want to try a short game test? Message me. Please don't put your name in this form."

**What to work out from the answers:**
- How many people answered (write it as n = 27, for example)
- Questions 3 and 4 side by side: how many play less now than at school
- The top 3 reasons in question 6
- For questions 9 and 12, how many chose 4 or 5
- For question 11, how many chose each answer

Don't turn the numbers into percentages with fewer than 20 answers. Write "9 of 14 people" instead.

## 2. One-to-one interviews

**Who to ask.** Pick 5 to 8 people who are different from each other:
- someone who played a team sport at school and stopped
- someone who never liked PE
- someone who still plays regularly
- the friend who usually organises things
- someone who lives in a hall, and someone who lives at home
- people from different years

**How.**
- Meet in person if you can, for 20 to 30 minutes.
- Take notes in 4 columns: **Says** (exact words, in quotation marks), **Does** (what they actually did), **Thinks** (what they believe), **Feels** (what emotions you heard).
- Write up your notes the same day, while you still remember the details.

**Questions.** The bullets under each question are follow-ups to use only if they don't come up on their own.

*Warm-up (2 minutes)*
1. What are you studying, and what year are you in?
2. What does a normal weekday evening look like for you?

*Then and now*

3. Think back to secondary school. What sports or active games did you play, and how often?
   - Who organised them? Where did you play? Who did you play with?
4. How is that different now, if at all?
   - If it's different: when did it change? Tell me what happened.
5. When did you last play a sport or active game with friends? Tell me about that day, from the first message to going home.

*Trying to play now*

6. Think of the last time someone said "we should play sometime". What happened after that?
   - Who replied? Who went quiet? Where did it stop?
7. How easy or hard is it to get a game going now? What makes it that way?
   - Follow up on what they mention: finding a time, a place, enough people, booking, money, energy.
8. Have you ever decided not to join a game? What was going through your mind?
9. Tell me about a time you played with people who were much better than you. How did it feel?
10. Have you ever been hurt playing a sport?
11. Does the chance of getting hurt change what you play? How?

*Free time and the phone*

12. What did you do last Saturday evening? How did you decide?
13. Think of the last evening you spent mostly on your phone. How did you feel afterwards?
14. If they're happy to share: what was your screen time last week? What do you think of that number?

*Looking ahead (don't describe your idea)*

15. If playing with friends were as easy as you'd like, what would it look like? Who would be there, how many people, and where?
16. What would make you say no straight away to an invitation to play?

*Wrap-up*

17. Is there anything I should have asked you?
18. Who else should I talk to?

## 3. Friend-group session

Do this with 3 to 5 friends who already know each other. It shows how a group really plans, which people often describe differently in an interview.

1. **Plan a game, now (10 minutes).** Say: "Imagine you want to play something together this weekend. Use your group chat to plan it, as you normally would." Don't help. Note:
   - who starts
   - who goes quiet
   - where it gets stuck
   - how long it takes
   - whether it ends with a time and place
2. **Talk about it (10 minutes).**
   - Is that what usually happens?
   - Who usually organises? How do the others feel about that?
   - What's the last game this group actually played? What made that one happen?
3. **Sort the reasons (10 minutes).** Give them cards with the reasons from survey question 6. Ask the group to agree on an order, from most true for this group to least true. Listen to the arguments. Their reasons tell you more than the final order.

## 4. Freeze playtest

Use the website on a laptop: https://isdn1001.lbzeng.com/freeze/.

**Set up.**
- First, run the checks in "What still needs testing" in `game/README.md` yourself, because the body tracking hasn't been tried with a real camera yet.
- Use Chrome or Edge.
- A room with clear space: about 3 metres for up to 5 players, and 3 to 4 metres for 6.
- The laptop on a table at about waist height.
- Normal room lights.
- Clear away chairs and bags.

**What to say.** "This is a game I'm testing. It's the game being tested, not you. I won't explain how it works, so we can see whether the screen makes sense." Then let them play. Only help if they're stuck for more than a minute, and note what they were stuck on.

**What to watch and write down:**

| What | How to note it |
|---|---|
| Time from opening the page to round 1 | Minutes and seconds |
| Did everyone in the room join in? | Note anyone who watched instead, and why |
| Did everyone get a name tag, and keep it when people moved? | Yes or no, and when it went wrong |
| Hands up: did raised hands get a yellow ring? | Yes or no |
| Copy the pose: did the head rings turn teal when people had the pose? | Note which poses failed |
| Did holding still feel fair? | Note any "but I didn't move!" |
| Did "Slow down" appear, and was it right? | When it appeared |
| Laughing, cheering, groaning | Roughly how often, and at what |
| Confusion | What they said, and on which screen |
| Anything that felt unsafe | What happened |
| Scores for each game | From the end screen |

**Questions after playing:**
1. Which game did you like most? Why?
2. What was confusing?
3. Did anyone feel left out? When?
4. Did anyone feel bad at it? When?
5. Would you play this again? When and where?
6. How would you describe it to a friend?
7. What would you change first?

Then ask each player to rate these on their own, before they talk about it, from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Report how many chose 4 or 5, not averages.
- It was fun.
- It was easy to start.
- I felt part of the team.
- I'd like to play again.

If the tracking goes wrong (missed players, wrong tags, poses not counted), note exactly what happened and when. The settings to change are in `game/js/pose.js` and `game/js/rounds.js`, as the game's README explains.

## After: from notes to personas

1. **Write up.** Type the notes from every interview and session into one document. Give each person a code (P1, P2…) instead of their name.
2. **One finding per note.** Write each finding or quote on its own sticky note, or a card in a Miro or FigJam board. Mark it with the person's code.
3. **Group them.** Put notes that say similar things together, then name each group in a short sentence, such as "Nobody wants to be the one who organises". This is an affinity map.
4. **Count.** For each group, count how many different people it came from. A theme from 1 person is a lead. A theme from 4 people is a finding.
5. **Personas.** Build 2 or 3 personas, each from at least 2 real people. Possible types (let the data decide):
   - the lapsed team player, like Mandy
   - the student who never felt sporty
   - the organiser who's tired of organising
6. **Empathy map.** For each persona: Says, Does, Thinks and Feels, with real quotes in Says.
7. **Journey map.** Follow one persona from wanting to play to playing, step by step:
   - wants to play
   - suggests it
   - finds a time
   - finds a place
   - gets enough people
   - turns up
   - plays
   - afterwards

   For each step, write what they do, what they feel, and where they give up.
8. **Update the problem.** Compare your findings with the pain points, problem statement and How Might We in `research/sport-background.md`. Change them where the data says something different. Then check the How Might We against the lecturers' rules in `docs/course-brief.md`.

**Persona template:**

| Part | What to write |
|---|---|
| Name and photo | Made up. Say on the slide that the persona is built from real interviews. |
| About them | Year, programme, where they live |
| Play history | What they played at school, and what changed |
| A real quote | From an interview, with the person's code |
| Goals | What they want from their free time |
| Frustrations | What gets in the way, in their words |
| What they do instead | And how they feel about it |
| What would make them play | From the interviews, not from your idea |
| Built from | The codes of the people it's based on, for example P2, P5 and P7 |
