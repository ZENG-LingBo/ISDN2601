## 4. Why group planning fails

| Reason | Evidence | How it shows up in a group chat |
|---|---|---|
| **No owner** | Diffusion of responsibility: the more people present, the less each one feels responsible to act. Darley & Latané (1968), *JPSP*, 8(4), 377–383, [doi:10.1037/h0025589](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0025589). | Five people “like” the idea; each assumes someone else will pick the date. |
| **No time, no place** | A plan that says *when* and *where* (an “implementation intention”) makes people much more likely to follow through: d = 0.65 across 94 studies, 8,000+ people. Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006), *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology*, 38, 69–119. Later reviews find smaller but still positive effects. | “Soon”, “after midterms” and “next week” are goals, not plans. |
| **Fear of pushing** | Liking gap (Boothby et al., 2018); people undervalue reaching out (Liu et al., 2022); students think peers are more socially busy than they are (Whillans et al., 2017). | “If I chase everyone, I’ll look desperate.” |
| **Cheap “yes”, easy relief** | 77% say yes to things they don’t want (Givi & Kirk, 2023); 56% of under-30s often regret plans (YouGov, 2022); 1 in 3 often feel relieved at a cancellation (YouGov, 2019). | Agreeing costs nothing, so it commits no one. |
| **Friendships need time** | About 50 hours together to become casual friends, about 90 to become friends, 200+ to become close friends. Hall (2019), *Journal of Social and Personal Relationships*, 36(4), 1278–1296, [doi:10.1177/0265407518761225](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407518761225). The university’s summary gives ranges of 40–60 and 80–100 hours. | Every dead plan is time the friendship never gets. |

**Limits of this evidence.** The loneliness data includes Hong Kong. The flaking and relief
data comes from UK and US polls; no Hong Kong flaking survey was found. Several polls are
online panels, and two (Yubo, Sky Mobile) are commercial and are not used on the slides. The
next step in a real project is to interview HKUST students to confirm the pattern locally.

## 5. Choosing what to do (added 26 Nov 2026)

**The insight.** Even when friends want to meet, choosing *what* to do stalls. Most plans
default to a meal. Some friends can't eat spicy food, have allergies or other food limits, are
saving money, or don't want to eat at all. They often stay quiet so they won't seem difficult,
and say 是但 ("whatever"). The group ends up at the same dinner, or doesn't meet.

This started as an observation from the student's own friend groups. It hasn't been checked
with interviews yet. The sources below support each part of it.

| Finding | Source | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| A group can agree to a plan that most of its members don't want, because each person thinks the others want it and nobody says otherwise. Harvey names this the Abilene paradox, after a family trip that none of them wanted to take. | Harvey, J. B. (1974). The Abilene paradox: The management of agreement. *Organizational Dynamics, 3*(1), 63–80. [doi:10.1016/0090-2616(74)90005-9](https://doi.org/10.1016/0090-2616(74)90005-9) | An essay with case stories, not an experiment. Used as the name for the pattern. Pages and DOI checked through a citing article; the paper itself wasn't opened. |
| Students believed they were less comfortable with campus drinking than the average student. Most students thought this, so the "average student" they pictured didn't exist. Feeling out of step went with feeling less connected to campus. | Prentice, D. A., & Miller, D. T. (1993). Pluralistic ignorance and alcohol use on campus. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 64*(2), 243–256. [doi:10.1037/0022-3514.64.2.243](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.64.2.243) | 4 studies with US university students. The research evidence that a group can wrongly think everyone else is happy with the default. Kept in this file; not on the slides. |
| People with food restrictions felt lonelier than people without them, partly because they worried that others would judge them or that they'd hold up the group's food choice. When people with restrictions remembered a meal where everyone could share the food, the feeling went away. | Woolley, K., Fishbach, A., & Wang, R. M. (2020). Food restriction and the experience of social isolation. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 119*(3), 657–671. [doi:10.1037/pspi0000223](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000223) · [Cornell news](https://news.cornell.edu/node/316726) | Several studies, including experiments, with US adults. Checked through the university's news release and science-news reports; the paper wasn't opened. The closest research to this insight. Used on the process deck's insight slide and empathy map. |
| In group discussions, people talk more about what everyone already knows than about what only one person knows. | Stasser, G., & Titus, W. (1985). Pooling of unshared information in group decision making. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 48*(6), 1467–1478. | Lab study. A private food limit is "unshared information", so it's the least likely thing to come up. |
| **44%** of people with a food allergy who answered the survey wouldn't always tell the person serving them in a restaurant or café. Teenagers were the most likely to worry about making a fuss or feeling embarrassed. | Food Standards Scotland with Young Scot (2020): [news release](https://www.foodstandards.gov.scot/news-and-alerts/new-campaign-to-encourage-young-people-to-talk-about-food-allergies) · [survey results](https://www.foodstandards.gov.scot/science-and-evidence/food-allergies-survey-results) | Self-selected online survey in Scotland. Shows that a meal is the default plan. Taken during the pandemic. Checked through search results; the page itself couldn't be opened from this environment. |

**Not found.** No study of how often friend groups in Hong Kong choose food, or how often
someone stays quiet about a food or money limit. No Hong Kong figure for food allergies in
adults (the Hong Kong studies found are about children). These are questions for the interviews.

**Searched but not used.** An IFIC (2024) figure that two-thirds of US Gen Z follow a diet was
only found through trade press, with an unclear age range. A Doves Farm survey on food
intolerance and social invitations is commercial. Neither is on the slides.

## 6. Design-thinking outputs (used in the deck)

### Target user

Hong Kong university students, especially Year 2 and up, in a small friend group (4–6 people)
formed in Year 1 (O-camp, hall, first-year classes) whose timetables have since split. They want
to meet more but rarely do.

**Why this group:** Year 1 gives ready-made chances to meet; by Year 2 those structures fade
while friendships are still young. Hall (2019) shows young friendships need many shared hours to
become close. This topic does not overlap with the classmates’ topics (remote workers, gamers,
gym goers, pet owners, urban families, walkers, young elderly, short waiting times).

### Persona: Mandy, 20, Year 2, HKUST

A composite persona built from the research above, not a real person. Four close friends from
Year 1 O-camp; now different timetables, part-time jobs and long commutes. She can't eat spicy
food, and money is tight. Quote: “We all want to meet. It just never happens.”

### Pain points

1. Plans stay vague: “soon”, “after midterms”.
2. Nobody wants to be the one who keeps asking.
3. Five timetables, jobs and commutes never line up.
4. Relief when the plan is cancelled, then guilt.
5. It's always dinner. She stays quiet about what she can't eat or afford, and says “whatever”. (Added 26 Nov, section 5.)

### Empathy map

| Says | Thinks |
|---|---|
| “Yes! Next week for sure.” · “是但, anything’s fine.” | “If I keep asking, I’ll seem desperate.” · “I don’t want to be the difficult one.” |
| **Does** | **Feels** |
| Waits for someone else to pick a day · Goes along with dinner again | Lonely and left out · Relieved when plans fall through, then guilty |

The pitch deck shows these eight items. Earlier versions also had “We should hang out soon!!”,
“Maybe they don’t really care”, “Someone else will pick a day”, “Likes the message, then goes
quiet” and “Scrolls friends’ stories alone”.

### Problem statement

Current version (pitch slide 7, updated 26 Nov):

> University friend groups want to spend their free time together, but their plans fall through
> in the group chat, and friends with food or money limits stay quiet. Instead, they spend it
> alone on their phones.

How it changed:

| Version | Text | Why it changed |
|---|---|---|
| 1. First draft | University friend groups want to spend time together, but their plans die in the group chat. No one owns the plan, it never gets a time or place, and cancelling feels like relief. So free time gets spent scrolling alone, and close friends drift apart. | Too long to say in one breath. “Die” and “drift apart” are metaphors. The causes belong on the earlier slides. |
| 2. Cycle 0 (text and design review) | The problem: university friend groups want to spend time together, but their plans fall through in the group chat. Instead, they spend their free time alone on their phones. | The pitch coach (cycle 7) found the pitch never said “problem statement”, and “free time”, the link to the leisure theme, came at the end. |
| 3. Cycle 7 (pitch coach) | Problem statement: university friend groups want to spend their free time together, but their plans fall through in the group chat. Instead, they spend it alone on their phones. | The new insight (section 5): even when plans move forward, friends with food or money limits stay quiet. |
| 4. Now | See above. | — |

### How might we

> **How might we turn “we should hang out” into real meetups for university friend groups, in
> order to keep their friendships close?**

Check against the lecturers’ rules:

| Rule | Met? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Specific user | Yes | University friend groups |
| Specific problem | Yes | “We should hang out” not turning into a meetup |
| No solution inside | Yes | No app, reminder, poll or feature is named |
| Broad enough for many ideas | Yes | Could lead to campus spaces, group rituals, social norms, wearables, IoT, games, events and more |
| Focus on desired outcome | Yes | Real meetups; friendships kept close |
| Positive wording | Yes | “keep their friendships close” (the draft’s “instead of fading” was negative) |
| Not only “providing information” | Yes | The outcome is a behaviour (meeting), not knowledge |
| Follows the template | Yes | How might we [turn “we should hang out” into real meetups] for [university friend groups] in order to [keep their friendships close]? |

After the new insight (section 5), the HMW was checked again and kept. “Real meetups” already
means meetups that people come to, and a dinner half the group can't enjoy isn't one. A
narrower HMW was added for ideas (the last one below).

Narrower HMWs for later ideation (not in the pitch):

- How might we make it feel natural for one friend to take the lead, for university friend groups, in order to move a plan forward?
- How might we help a friend group settle on a time and place quickly, for busy university students, in order to turn “soon” into a date?
- How might we make showing up feel better than the relief of cancelling, for university students, in order to keep them choosing to meet?
- How might we help university friend groups choose something everyone can enjoy, in order to include every friend without anyone having to speak up?

### Link to the course theme

The brief asks how leisure in 2030 moves from killing time to intentional, meaningful downtime,
with a trend toward live, in-person experiences. Free time that was meant to be shared with
friends turns into time scrolling alone. This problem sits exactly at that shift.
